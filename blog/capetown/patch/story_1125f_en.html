Cape Town, South Africa

A turquoise sea under twelve peaks,
and a lesson that cost twenty times the price

Visited October 31 – November 10, 2015

Route Incheon → Istanbul → Cape Town, and home again via Istanbul

Camps Bay

A sip of wine, a square of cherry chocolate, and a tuition fee twenty times the going rate, paid at a market in Istanbul.

To get those three things, I spent two days on planes.

Hello, I'm Yang Seungjin, an artist who stages ideas (생각을 연출하는 작가, saeng-gak-eul yeon-chul-ha-neun jak-ga). Today I'm taking you back to the autumn of 2015, to Cape Town, the city at the very bottom of the African continent. The sea is pool-blue and the mountain looks sliced flat with a knife. I've already done all the hard parts of getting there, so just sit back and enjoy the view. The good bits, we'll see together.

It was a long time ago, yet opening these photos again, it feels as vivid as today.

Flight & sea

To see this one view, it took two days on planes.

①

Two days of flying

Departure. How many of my meals those catering boxes would cover, I had no idea yet

Autumn 2015, a work trip. Incheon to Istanbul, change planes, then across the equator to the tip of Africa.

‘Change planes’ sounds quick. In practice it's two days.

On a long-haul flight, the first thing to surrender isn't your stamina — it's your neck. Doze off for a moment and you wake with your head folded to one side; sit down in a transit-airport chair and now your back starts filing complaints. At the gate for the second flight I made myself a quiet promise: next time, the neck pillow goes in first.

I'm handing that promise to you now, before you need it.

Cape Town International Airport. Even the roof curves like a wave

The luggage trolleys got here before I did

Arrived.

After two days, the outside air smelled dry and light. November in the southern hemisphere is early summer. Back in Korea people were pulling out autumn jackets; here it was short sleeves.

The first thing I checked on arrival wasn't the view. It was whether my neck still turned both ways.

It did, thankfully. Tuition fee no. 1, paid in neck muscles.

②

Atlantic light

Bags down, straight to the sea. Only one thing on today's list: an Atlantic sunset.

The aloes turn into silhouettes, and the sea breaks into gold

On this coast the sun sets into the sea. Aloe leaves go black against it, and between the fence posts the whole surface of the water breaks into gold. An evening with outlines as clear as a woodblock print.

Every tide pool held a piece of the sunset, so I left the sun out and looked straight down at the pools · Look at: the pool inside the circle

Bicycles, palm trees, white apartments

Sunset next to the outdoor gym. Take away the sea and it's my neighbourhood park

In Korea this would be the riverside walking path every neighbourhood has. The only difference: where the stream should be, there's the Atlantic.

Lovely — but too bright to look at for long

This sunlight is strong, though. Stand there taking in the view and you'll soon be squinting, and on the shadeless coastal road your arms and the back of your neck heat up fast. It's that sting you feel the day after a whole day at the beach without sunscreen.

Camps Bay. No filter — it really is this colour

Then, on a day the sky cleared completely, I stood on the hill above Camps Bay.

Twelve peaks stand shoulder to shoulder, looking down at the sea. At their feet the water shifts from turquoise to navy again and again. Picture the jade-green sea of Hyeopjae (협재) beach on Jeju Island, spread out ten times wider, with the ridges of Seoraksan (설악산) standing behind it like a folding screen. Two days of flying, and the tiredness melted away right here.

Twelve Apostles, eighteen peaksThese peaks are called the ‘Twelve Apostles,’ but when I counted, there were eighteen. If the numbers don't match, it isn't our eyes that are wrong.

A pause for coffee

Thomas William Bowler (1812–1869)

Choosing this photo, I set a cup of coffee down and sat back. While we're resting, here's the story of the man who painted this sea first.

When I looked into it, someone had painted this sea about 160 years before me. Thomas William Bowler: a young Englishman who stepped off the ship in 1834 as the servant of the Cape's newly appointed astronomer, and who then spent nearly 35 years painting almost nothing but this land and sea.

I found his watercolour ‘Camps Bay, Cape of Good Hope’ — round granite boulders, a tide pool, the peaks running behind. The boulders in my photo lie in the same spot, in the same shape. A model that hasn't changed its pose in 160 years. Landscape may be the only genre where the model never ages.

I paint the sea a lot myself, so I did the maths. He spent paint and a whole day on this scene; I spent one second with a phone. What's still here 160 years later is the day, not the second. So these days I try to just stand and look for ten seconds before I press the shutter.

Standing on this hill and laying that 160-year-old watercolour over the view in your mind might just be one of the small pleasures of this trip.

The tree seemed to tell me which way the wind blows, so I shot it from the side to show the lean · Look at: the trunk leaning one way

A lone tree on the hillside lawn leans toward the sea. Before anyone tells you that the wind here always blows from one direction, the tree already has.

The water this clear, and I never once put my feet in

For all that clear water, I didn't dip a toe. A cold current coming up from the Antarctic side runs past this coast, so even on a turquoise day the water is far colder than it looks. Like putting your feet in a mountain stream in summer and pulling them out after three seconds. On the Atlantic, the sea you look at and the sea you get into are two different seas.

 

Food — wine and chocolate in the Constantia valley

Winery

A sip of wine, then a square of chocolate.

③

The winery

On the way toward Table Mountain — honestly, this was the real destination of the day. Groot Constantia, the oldest wine estate in South Africa.

Vineyards all the way up to the foothills. There's even a playground; a family picnic spot

White walls, a rounded gable, a long pond. The building balances itself, left and right

The farm dates from 1685. White plastered walls topped with rounded gables, the ‘Cape Dutch’ style of the Dutch settlement era, and a long pond in front that reflects the building once more, upside down.

An old grape press. A butterfly painting hangs behind it

A painting of a Cape Town street lined with colourful houses

Inside: old presses and paintings.

The cellar. The barrels lie on and on, out of sight

I liked how the red wine-stained bands ran at the same height from barrel to barrel, so I looked down at an angle · Look at: the red band on the barrels, left photo

Down the stairs is the cellar. In a long, low-lit room, oak barrels lie at the same angle without end, each with a red band where the wine has soaked into its belly. It's the reassuring feeling of seeing rows of crocks on a jangdokdae (장독대), the terrace where Korean families keep their earthenware jars of fermented sauces. Except these crocks hold wine, not doenjang (된장, soybean paste).

After one lap of the cellar, my mouth wanted something to do. And as it happened, the tastiest part of the whole trip was waiting: a wine and chocolate pairing.

It's simple. A sip of wine, a square of chocolate. Wine's dryness comes from the same compounds (tannins) that make your tongue curl when you bite into an unripe persimmon. When chocolate steps into that dry spot, its sweetness sometimes covers the astringency and sometimes lifts the wine's aroma right up. Koreans have fixed pairings — makgeolli (막걸리, rice wine) with pajeon (파전, scallion pancake), soju with grilled pork belly — and this was a drinks table with its partners already decided, too.

What surprised me was the chocolate side. To me, chocolate had always been one flavour: ‘chocolate.’ But chocolate itself branching into flavours, like cherry, with a wine matched to each one — that was a first. Every square, every sip, was a small surprise.

Until then I thought cherries were the ones that sit on top of the cake. That day, the cherry had gone inside the chocolate.

Out of the cellar, back into the sun. A big tree's shade over the vines

Folded parasols. Next time I'll come on a day they're open

A couple of sips of wine plus the sunshine, and before I'd even gone up Table Mountain, my mood had already reached the summit.

 

Scenery — a flat mountain and the harbour below

Table Mountain

Its top is flat as if sliced with a knife — hence Table Mountain.

④

Table Mountain

Now it's the main character's turn.

A mountain at the end of the road. In this city you just look up and there it is

Same mountain from every angle

In Cape Town, whichever street you turn your head down, the mountain is in the frame. Lose your way, find the mountain, and you have your bearings. This city hung its signpost about 1,000 m above sea level.

It's like using Bukhansan (북한산) to find your way around Seoul — except here the summit is flat, as if someone ran an iron over it.

The lookout. The city fills right up to the foot of the mountain

Opposite: pointed Lion's Head

I climbed to a hillside lookout facing the mountain. The sun stings but the wind is cold, so you come up in short sleeves and soon find your arms folded. When the southeaster blows hard, a white cloud spreads over the summit like a cloth, and locals really do call it the ‘Tablecloth.’ The weather even sets the table to match the mountain's name.

Everyone stepped back from the frame, so I stood in the same spot and lined the mountain up right inside it · Look at: Table Mountain inside the frame

At the lookout stands a giant yellow frame. That's all it is, a frame — yet people stop, step back, and choose where to stand to fit the mountain inside it. It does exactly what a frame does in a gallery, only in front of a mountain. As an artist, I was a little jealous of that idea.

The fastest way to the top is the cableway, opened in 1929. It closes on very windy days, so it's easier on the mind to check the weather before picking a time.

Culture — things carved by hand, gifts that cross borders

Souvenirs & customs

To the giver it's a gesture; to the receiving country's customs, it's an import.

⑤

Souvenirs and customs

Halfway through the trip, time to pick out gifts.

One entire shelf of the souvenir shop is giraffes. The shelves were so crowded that I brought out only the things my eye kept returning to. Each one is carved by hand, so no two faces match, and my hand kept reaching not for the neatest one but for ‘the one that's different.’ In front of the zebra hide and the horn crafts, I stopped for a moment too.

Import cautionSouvenirs made from animal materials such as hides and horn may need export and import permits depending on the species. Ask the shop about the species and paperwork before you buy, and check your home country's customs and quarantine guidance before you fly back.

And then I was the one stopped — somewhere I never expected.

It was the fountain pens I'd brought from Korea as gifts. Each cost over 30,000 won (about 25 US dollars), and at South African customs I paid 10% duty on every one. The moment a carefully wrapped gesture got a tax stamped on it.

To the giver it's a gesture; to the receiving country's customs, it's an import.

So those pens said hello to South African customs before they ever met the people they were for. Tuition fee no. 2: 10% per pen.

The first rand notes I held were stiffer than I expected. A green R10 with a rhino, a red R50 with a lion — and the bigger the value, the longer the note. Taking out rand notes to pay the duty, I noticed every note had the same face: Nelson Mandela. Curious, I looked it up — since 2012 every banknote carries Mandela on the front and one of the ‘Big Five’ on the back: rhino, elephant, lion, buffalo, leopard. The rand itself was born in 1961, the year South Africa set aside the British pound and became a republic. Going further back, the Cape was ruled by Britain from 1806; under the Union of South Africa (from 1910), a British dominion, segregation laws such as the Natives Land Act (1913) piled up, and the National Party government that came to power in 1948 hardened them into the system called apartheid. The man who spent 27 years in prison fighting that system was right there on the note I was handing over.

Next time I cross a border with gifts, I'll check the other country's duty-free allowance first. Several of the same item can apparently be treated as commercial goods.

A short rest

Esther Mahlangu (1935–)

Soothing my customs-bruised heart in front of the giraffe shelf, I thought of someone.

When I looked into it, there's an artist called Esther Mahlangu. She learned the bold geometric patterns that South Africa's Ndebele women have painted on the outer walls of their homes from her mother and grandmother at around ten, and carried them onto canvas and metal.

In 1991 she painted an entire BMW 525i in Ndebele patterns — the first woman, and the first non-Western artist, to make a BMW Art Car. After 32 years abroad, the car came home and was shown at Iziko in Cape Town.

She has no direct connection to this shop. Still, standing in front of giraffes with no two patterns alike and picturing a line that travelled from her grandmother's mud wall to a German car might be another way to enjoy a souvenir shop. There are a lot of hand-drawn lines in my own paintings, so when I think of her story, I draw the next one a little more boldly.

About the BMW Art Car (official)About the artist (Wikipedia)

And in this shop I picked gifts for my colleagues: little animal figures carved from stone, like hippos, and handmade pencils of wood shaped like animals.

Stone, they were. That becomes a problem later.

 

Second country — Türkiye, Istanbul's market and flavours

Istanbul

I didn't buy it dear. I learned it dear.

⑥

Through Istanbul

The check-in hall. Numbered counters stretching on and on

The way home was also via Istanbul — another two-day journey.

On the check-in scale, the stone hippos made their move. They were absurdly heavy. In the end I used Korean Air miles to raise my baggage allowance. Tuition fee no. 3, paid in weight.

Still, one bit of luck came my way. The Istanbul layover was so long that the airline provided a hotel and meals. I caught my breath at a hotel near the airport, then decided to carve out the remaining hours and head into the city.

The tram map. All I could read were the numbers and the colours

I took out local cash at an ATM and got on the tram. All I could read were numbers and colours, so my map app and my phone battery were my guides.

Getting cash from the ATM took a minute.

The lira notes were even more colourful: green for 20, blue for 100, and suddenly my wallet looked like a paint box. The lira notes from the ATM also had one face on every front: Mustafa Kemal Atatürk, who founded the republic. Looking it up, Türkiye knocked six zeros off its currency in 2005 — a million lira became one. Mandela, who ended apartheid, on South Africa's notes; Atatürk, who founded the republic, on Türkiye's. Both countries carry the face of a fresh start in their wallets. And a few of those Atatürks were about to become my tuition in the bazaar.

If your layover is longTurkish Airlines runs a free city tour (Touristanbul) for its own passengers with a 6–24 hour connection at Istanbul Airport.

⑦

The Grand Bazaar — a twenty-times tuition fee

The moment you pass through the arched gate, even the sound changes. The Grand Bazaar. It began as a textile market in 1455 and now has 61 covered streets and over 4,000 shops. Put a roof over the whole of Seoul's Namdaemun Market (남대문시장), add 560 years, and you might be close.

A carpet display is one giant sample book

Lokum, baklava, spices stacked like towers. The colours hit me before the prices did, so I shot the display head-on · Look at: the ₺ (lira) price tags in the right photo

The sweet shops have lokum, and baklava — dozens of thin layers of pastry soaked in syrup — set out by the tray. Baklava looks like yakgwa (약과), the Korean honey cookie, opened out into a hundred layers. This market sells colour before it sells anything else.

And here I bought a souvenir and perfume for my mother.

The moment the shopkeeper saw me, it was ‘Bro, bro — Korea? Brother country!’ (형제의 나라, hyeong-je-ui na-ra) and with that one line my guard melted away. Tea appeared, perfume bottles were opened one by one and dabbed on the back of my hand, and somewhere in between, the price had already been decided.

Later I found out I'd paid about twenty times the going rate.

Not twice. Twenty times. The biggest tuition fee of the trip. I didn't buy it dear; I learned it dear. It was the price of learning, in my bones, that haggling ‘starts’ at the first price they name.

One more thing. Later I found out that Türkiye calls more than nine countries its ‘brother country.’ The ‘bro’ you hear in the market is a shopkeeper's business friendliness. It's nice to let it warm your heart — just no need to let it open your wallet too.

What I'll do next timeHear the first price as the starting line. Ask two or three shops what the same item costs first. Show a little less love for the thing you want; pretending to walk away is half of haggling, they say. Keep cash in small notes, and in crowded lanes wear the bag in front.

But this story has a twist.

My mother's gift was something she had wanted for a long time: a ceramic figure of a man in a traditional Turkish skirt, dancing. She was truly delighted, and that figure is still dancing at home today. I paid twenty times the price, but it has been earning its keep every single day for eleven years — do the maths and it wasn't a bad deal.

I also brought home Turkish delight, lokum. It's starch and sugar boiled down and set, so the texture lands somewhere between soft yanggaeng (양갱, red-bean jelly) and chapssal-tteok (찹쌀떡, sticky rice cake). The problem was the sweetness — like eating jocheong (조청, rice syrup) straight off the spoon. One bite of the sweet that looked so pretty in the shop window and I choked on it. That ordeal was shared out evenly among the whole family.

Even now, whenever someone brings out something sweet at a holiday gathering, that story comes up. A story to tell for the rest of my life — bought in Istanbul.

Wait — let the sweetness settle

Osman Hamdi Bey (1842–1910)

While the choking settles, here's a painting that has nothing to do with the bazaar, yet captures me on that day most precisely.

When I looked into it, in 2004 one painting with five tortoises in it sold for 3.5 million dollars: Osman Hamdi Bey's ‘The Tortoise Trainer’ (1906). He was a painter who also founded the Istanbul Archaeology Museums and, in 1882, opened a school of fine arts. The painting now hangs in Istanbul's Pera Museum.

An old man with a drum tries to train the tortoises, and the tortoises just eat leaves. It's often read as a jab at slow reform.

On the day I was learning to haggle in the bazaar, I was more tortoise than old man. I heard every lesson; my speed just couldn't keep up.

Thinking back to the day I learned haggling ‘slowly,’ at twenty times the price, those tortoises feel like old friends. Dropping into the Pera Museum after the bazaar to meet them in person might be one more pleasure of Istanbul. Even a slow tortoise learns in the end.

⑧

My taste buds come home

Whenever I see French fries, the ones I had in Australia and South Africa come back first — soggy outside, mealy inside. A trauma spread by Britain's misguided palate, you might say. Where the crunch should have been, it felt like laundry that hadn't dried.

So I arrived in Istanbul with a cautious palate from the very start.

At the juice stands near the bazaar, halved pomegranates and oranges are piled up by the crate and pressed into juice right in front of you. To look at, it was the most delicious-looking scene of the whole trip.

The sweet-and-sour first sip reminded me of omija-cha (오미자차, five-flavour berry tea), but bits of pomegranate pith kept getting caught, so I had to spit after every mouthful like spitting out watermelon seeds. Istanbul's food was dazzling; my taste buds, apparently, were still going through departure procedures at Incheon Airport. The food did nothing wrong — my tongue just arrived late.

At times like that, the place to lean on is, surprisingly, the supermarket fruit aisle.

A pear I picked up at a Cape Town supermarket. A meal that never fails

That's what the pear from a Cape Town supermarket was. Instead of the crisp, watery bite of a Korean pear, a sweetness that gives way softly, like a ripe hongsi (홍시, soft persimmon). Secure one meal with supermarket fruit and bread, and you can enjoy the local food as an experiment, with nothing riding on it.

 

History — the lines left on a city's map

Epilogue

The scene that stayed with me longest isn't actually in any photo.

⑨

Epilogue

The big wheel that has stood at the harbour since around the 2010 World Cup

One night in Cape Town, the big wheel at the V&A Waterfront turned slowly with white lights along its spokes. A Ferris wheel, after one full turn, comes back to where it started.

Istanbul's old walls. A city that leaves even the ruins as they are

On the way home, Istanbul's sky was grey. A crumbling tower of the Theodosian Walls, completed in 439, stood on the grass. A city that keeps even its broken places as history.

But the scene that stayed with me longest isn't actually in any photo.

It was the day I arrived in Cape Town, on the road from the airport into the city. Outside the car window, houses pieced together from boards stretched on and on. Houses on the hills with a view of the sea, and that neighbourhood by the road. Two utterly different landscapes side by side in one city — and on that road, I understood clearly who lives in those shacks.

Townships and apartheidThe N2 highway linking the airport and the city runs past a number of townships. In the apartheid (racial segregation) era, under the Group Areas Act of 1950, residents who weren't white were pushed out of the city centre and made to live apart in these areas. The law ended in 1991 and apartheid with the democratic election of 1994, but the townships are still where they were.

An artist tied to this road

Peter Clarke (1929–2014)

When I looked into it, he was a painter and poet born in Simon's Town. In 1972, under that same Group Areas Act, he was forced to move to Ocean View. For nearly 60 years he recorded South African society in woodcuts and collage, and came to be called a ‘quiet chronicler.’

The inner life of the neighbourhoods that flashed past my car window — he kept it, not in photographs, but in prints.

About the artist (South African History Online)

On that road I became certain: if Korea had not won its independence from the Japanese Empire, that landscape could have been ours.

In a land split into those who rule and those who are ruled, who lives on the hill with the sea view and who lives in the roadside shack is decided before anyone is born. Even after the law that drew the line is gone, the line stays on the city's map for a long time.

Back home, I settled the tuition bill.

Going by the receipt it was a losing trip — but count what I received, and it came out ahead.

Even the hard parts, all of it, became stories I'll keep telling for the rest of my life.

I thought the photos were why such an old trip felt as vivid as today. Looking again, it was the receipt.

If I go back to see that turquoise sea under the twelve peaks, this time I'd like to take the airport road slowly, too.

The neck pillow comes with me, and I won't order the fries. …Probably.
